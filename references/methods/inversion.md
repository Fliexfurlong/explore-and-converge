# Assumption inversion: find a defensible opposite premise

Use when a design, sequence, division of work, or usage pattern is taken for granted. Examine the assumptions supporting the route rather than opposing it for its own sake. Unlike provocation, inversion needs an account of how the opposite premise could work; provocation may temporarily be unrealistic to extract a principle.

## 1. Find load-bearing assumptions

Extract premises whose failure would change the current route. Select relevant dimensions such as user, value, channel, timing, who pays, or who performs the work. Do not pad the set with paraphrases of one assumption.

Distinguish:

- Confirmed hard constraints: still boundaries for actionable recommendations; inversion cannot cancel them.
- User preferences or explicit goals: consequences of changing them may be discussed, but the change is not automatically approved.
- Claims about facts or effects: state evidence and uncertainty; examine consequential claims first.
- Exploratory settings: use to generate directions, then return to real conditions.

The user does not need the whole checklist. Expand only items that affect direction.

## 2. Form a credible opposite

Preserve the goal while changing a default implementation premise. State a version a reasonable person could choose rather than an obviously unreasonable straw man.

For example, invert "information is always better when immediate" into "some information is more useful when delayed and batched." Specify the information and why batching helps. "Nobody ever receives any information" skips the mechanism that would make the inversion work.

An inversion need not reverse everything. Change the actor, timing, or applicable population to produce a bounded opposite. If only a word changes and behavior stays the same, look for a more load-bearing premise.

## 3. Find applicability conditions and distinguish evidence states

For each inversion worth retaining, ask:

1. Under which populations, task stages, and resource conditions could it work?
2. Which problem did the original premise solve, and what mechanism now performs that function?
3. Which changed fact would make the inversion fail?

Known real-world examples can provide leads; verify specific examples or effect claims. Lack of a known example does not make a direction necessarily invalid. Retain it as a conditional hypothesis with a minimal validation if appropriate. Do not invent claims that an industry has proved it, or generalize one example into universal effectiveness.

Seeds without a mechanism may remain in a development area, but should not become recommendations until that gap is filled. Explain and set aside branches that violate hard constraints, leave the goal, or have no discernible mechanism.

## 4. Develop the inversion into a direction

For retained inversions, specify who does what and when, which causal link changes, and benefits and costs. Separate a design opportunity from a prediction of effectiveness.

Some inversions apply only under particular conditions and can share roles with the original route. Others are prerequisites or complementary mechanisms rather than either/or choices. Compare when selection is requested; for divergence, retain candidates and gaps.

## 5. Check whether the choice space actually opened

- Do retained inversions still share an unexamined premise? Reframe if necessary.
- Are all directions just "less," "more," or "smarter," without changing roles, order, or mechanism? Choose another load-bearing assumption.
- Has "users prefer it" become "it works better," or unfamiliarity become effectiveness? Restore the distinction between preference and evidence.
- Was the original route excluded prematurely to appear bold? Finding the original premise still reasonable is a valid result.

Require no fixed number of successful or failed branches. Record actual reasons for unproductive branches and conditions for reopening them. Stop when no new information can advance the judgment.

## Brief example

Hypothetical setting: a writer always clears the inbox before writing, continually delaying creative work.

- **Original assumption:** "Every message must be handled before focused work can begin." This is a work-sequence assumption to examine, not a confirmed hard constraint.
- **Defensible inversion:** "Finish a writing segment before handling nonurgent messages." Conditions include recognizing genuinely urgent messages and respecting promised response requirements.
- **Mechanism:** replace the external condition of an empty inbox with a controllable time block for starting creative work. This does not prove writing output will increase.
- **A condition that cannot simply be removed:** immediate-response duties on a particular day remain duties. Consider another time, division of work, or a different task.
- **Minimal validation:** compare actual starts and urgent-response performance across a few work sessions before deciding whether to retain the change.

This is a method example, not a schedule or experiment already authorized by the user.
